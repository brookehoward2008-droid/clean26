WEEK 8 · COLLEGE ESSAY

What the Margin Kept

A quarter of rules, research, fragments, and finally hearing my own voice.

I came into this quarter
with a ruler in my hand,
measuring myself against rules
I could not always see.

In Business Communications,
I learned to hear the red pen
before it ever touched the page.
Format. Tone. Spacing.
One small thing out of place,
and I was already searching
for what I had done wrong.

Then this class asked me
what I thought,
and waited.

No trapdoor opened.
No voice corrected the shape
of the thought before it finished.
I could follow a question
past the obvious answer,
through research,
through doubt,
through the strange little hallway
where belief becomes evidence
and evidence changes belief.

I learned that facts can be a form of love.
A citation can be a flashlight.
A search bar can become a handrail
when the thing you are trying to understand
has your own blood in it,
or the name of someone you love.

Psychology.
Crypto chains.
Scholarship essays.
My own body translated into medical language.
Mental health resources for my son.
Poems that said something softly
a textbook could not.

My tabs never seemed to belong together,
but somehow, I did.

My thoughts still arrive in pieces.
A sentence first.
Then a question.
Then something I read days ago
comes back and sits beside it.
I used to sweep those pieces away
before anyone could see the mess.
Now I place them next to each other
and wait.

Sometimes the pattern appears.
Sometimes it does not.
Both are thinking.

That may be what changed most.
I stopped confusing polish
with intelligence.
I stopped believing a voice
had to sound distant
to be taken seriously.
I learned that emotion can carry evidence,
that structure can hold a wild thought
without strangling it,
and that uncertainty is not emptiness.
Sometimes it is the exact place
where a real idea begins.

I thought College Essay
would teach me how to sound educated.
Instead,
it taught me to hear
when I already did.

The margin is still there.
But it is not waiting to catch me.

It is white space now.
Room enough for the sentence
to turn around,
look back at me,
and recognize
its own voice.
